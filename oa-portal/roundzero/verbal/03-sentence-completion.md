# Sentence completion & cloze

TCS's updated verbal section is built around this — **20 sentence-completion questions** in one paper. It is the single highest-volume verbal format you will face.

## The two formats

- **Sentence completion** — one or two blanks in a standalone sentence
- **Cloze test** — a passage with 5–10 blanks, where context accumulates

---

## The method

**1. Read the whole sentence before looking at the options.** Reading up to the blank and stopping is how people get these wrong — the clue is very often *after* the gap.

**2. Predict your own word.** Before glancing at the options, decide roughly what belongs:  
positive or negative, and what part of speech. Then find the option closest to your guess.

**3. Find the signpost word.** Every completion sentence has one, and it tells you whether the two halves agree or contrast.

| Signpost | Relationship | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrast** | the halves oppose | but, although, though, however, yet, despite, in spite of, nevertheless, whereas, while, on the contrary, unlike |
| **Continuation** | the halves agree | and, moreover, furthermore, besides, similarly, likewise, in addition |
| **Cause / effect** | one produces the other | because, since, as, therefore, thus, hence, consequently, so that |
| **Example** | the second illustrates the first | for instance, such as, namely |
| **Concession then reversal** | admits, then overturns | admittedly… but, granted… still |

**4. Check both blanks.** In two-blank questions, eliminate on whichever blank you are more certain about — you rarely need both.

---

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — contrast

*Although the film received excellent reviews, it was a commercial ______.*

- Signpost: **Although** → contrast. Excellent reviews are positive, so the blank is
  negative.
- Prediction: "failure", "flop", "disaster".
- Options: (a) triumph (b) failure (c) sensation (d) breakthrough → **(b) failure**

### Example 2 — cause and effect

*The bridge was closed for repairs; ______, commuters had to take a longer route.*

- Signpost: semicolon plus an effect → **consequently / therefore**.
- Options: (a) however (b) consequently (c) nevertheless (d) although → **(b) consequently**

### Example 3 — two blanks

*Despite his ______ manner, he was widely regarded as one of the most ______ negotiators in the firm.*

- **Despite** → contrast between the two blanks.
- The second is positive ("most ___ negotiators" alongside "widely regarded"). So the first
  is something that *seems* at odds with being a good negotiator.
- Prediction: first = mild/quiet/unassuming; second = effective/formidable.
- Options: (a) aggressive … ruthless (b) diffident … formidable (c) confident … skilled
  (d) rude … popular
- (a) and (c) have no contrast between the halves. (d) is a contrast but "rude … popular"
  does not fit "negotiators". → **(b) diffident … formidable**

### Example 4 — continuation

*The new policy simplified the tax structure and ______ reduced compliance costs for small businesses.*

- Signpost: **and** → continuation, same direction.
- Prediction: "also", "additionally".
- Options: (a) nevertheless (b) additionally (c) conversely (d) regrettably → **(b)**

## Cloze tests

A passage with several blanks. Context builds, so:

- **Read the entire passage first**, blanks and all. Do not answer as you go.
- **Do the easy blanks first.** Each one you fill narrows the meaning for the rest.
- Watch for **grammatical** constraints, not just meaning — the blank may need a specific
  tense, a plural, or a preposition.
- Check the **tone** of the passage and stay consistent with it. A formal passage will not
  suddenly take a colloquial word.
- **Re-read the finished passage.** If any sentence now reads oddly, one of your choices is
  wrong.

---

## Grammar-driven blanks

Some blanks are decided by grammar alone, no meaning required:

| The blank follows | It needs |
|---|---|
| a preposition | a noun or a gerund (*good at **swimming***) |
| "one of the" | a plural noun |
| a countable singular | an article |
| *hardly / scarcely* | **when** (not *than*) |
| *no sooner* | **than** (not *when*) |
| *not only* | a matching *but also* |
| *lest* | **should** (*lest he **should** fail*) |
| *would rather* | a bare infinitive (*would rather **go***) |

> ✓ **No sooner** had he arrived **than** it began to rain.
> ✓ **Hardly** had he arrived **when** it began to rain.

That pair is asked directly and often.

## Practice set

#### Q1. Although the scheme was well designed, its implementation was ______.

(a) flawless (b) exemplary (c) haphazard (d) systematic  
*Although* → contrast with "well designed" → negative needed.  
**(c) haphazard**

#### Q2. The witness's account was so ______ that the lawyer could not shake it.

(a) vague (b) consistent (c) contradictory (d) hesitant  
"could not shake it" → the account held firm.  
**(b) consistent**

#### Q3. His arguments were ______; nobody could find a flaw in them.

(a) tenuous (b) cogent (c) verbose (d) ambiguous  
The semicolon explains the blank — no flaws → strong, convincing.  
**(b) cogent**

#### Q4. The minister refused to ______ the rumours, which only made them spread further.

(a) confirm (b) deny (c) circulate (d) initiate  
"only made them spread" → refusing to *deny* fuels a rumour.  
**(b) deny**

#### Q5. Despite his ______ appearance, he proved to be a ______ competitor.

(a) frail … formidable (b) strong … weak (c) neat … tidy (d) calm … composed  
*Despite* → contrast; (c) and (d) have no contrast, (b) reverses the sense.  
**(a) frail … formidable**

#### Q6. The new policy was ______ by the workers, who saw it as a threat to their jobs.

(a) welcomed (b) resisted (c) ignored (d) proposed  
"threat to their jobs" → opposition.  
**(b) resisted**

#### Q7. Since the evidence was ______, the court had no option but to acquit him.

(a) overwhelming (b) conclusive (c) inadequate (d) damning  
Acquittal follows weak evidence.  
**(c) inadequate**

#### Q8. The scientist was praised for her ______ approach, which left no detail unchecked.

(a) casual (b) meticulous (c) hasty (d) superficial  
"no detail unchecked" → extremely careful.  
**(b) meticulous**

#### Q9. He is so ______ that he believes every story he is told.

(a) credible (b) creditable (c) credulous (d) incredulous  
*Credulous* = too ready to believe. *Credible* = believable. Do not confuse them.  
**(c) credulous**

#### Q10. The two countries agreed to ______ their differences and resume trade.

(a) aggravate (b) reconcile (c) intensify (d) prolong  
"and resume trade" → settling differences.  
**(b) reconcile**

#### Q11. No sooner had the bell rung ______ the students rushed out.

(a) when (b) then (c) than (d) that  
*No sooner … than.*  
**(c) than**

#### Q12. The manager asked the team to work ______ to meet the deadline.

(a) diligent (b) diligence (c) diligently (d) diligentness  
The blank modifies the verb *work* → adverb.  
**(c) diligently**

### Cloze passage

*Fill blanks 13–17.*

> Remote work has fundamentally __(13)__ the way companies operate. What began as a
> temporary measure __(14)__ the pandemic has become a permanent feature for many
> organisations. __(15)__, the transition has not been without difficulty. Managers report
> that maintaining team cohesion is __(16)__ harder when colleagues rarely meet in person.
> __(17)__, most firms now offer some form of hybrid arrangement.

**Q13.** (a) altered (b) alter (c) altering (d) alteration  
After "has" a past participle is needed. **(a) altered**

**Q14.** (a) despite (b) during (c) although (d) between  
A period of time follows. **(b) during**

**Q15.** (a) Therefore (b) Moreover (c) However (d) Similarly  
The sentence reverses the positive tone before it. **(c) However**

**Q16.** (a) considerable (b) considerably (c) consider (d) consideration  
Modifies the adjective *harder* → adverb. **(b) considerably**

**Q17.** (a) Nevertheless (b) Consequently (c) Conversely (d) Meanwhile  
The hybrid arrangement is a *result* of the difficulty just described.  
**(b) Consequently**

> Notice that Q13 and Q16 were settled by **grammar alone** — no understanding of remote work
> required. In a cloze passage, always check whether the blank is a grammar slot before
> reasoning about meaning.

## Traps

- Reading only up to the blank. The decisive clue is frequently in the second half.
- Missing a contrast signpost and choosing a word in the wrong direction — this is the single
  biggest cause of errors here.
- Choosing the hardest word. Difficulty is not a signal of correctness.
- In two-blank questions, matching only the first blank and stopping.
- In cloze passages, answering blanks in order instead of easiest-first.
- Ignoring grammar: an option can be perfect in meaning and still be the wrong part of
  speech.

---

## Checkpoints

- [ ] I read the entire sentence before looking at any option
- [ ] I predict my own word before reading the options
- [ ] I identify the signpost word and know whether it signals contrast, continuation or cause
- [ ] I know the contrast list (although, despite, yet, whereas, however…) by sight
- [ ] In two-blank questions I eliminate using the blank I am surer about
- [ ] I read a cloze passage completely before filling anything
- [ ] I fill the easiest cloze blanks first and let them constrain the rest
- [ ] I check whether a blank is decided by grammar rather than meaning
- [ ] I know no sooner… than and hardly… when
- [ ] I re-read the completed passage to catch a choice that no longer fits
