# Error spotting & sentence correction

Accenture asks 3–4 of these; TCS, Infosys and Wipro all include them. The grammar sheet has the rules — this sheet is about the **scanning procedure** that finds the error in 20 seconds.

## The formats

| Format | What you do |
|---|---|
| **Error spotting** | sentence split into parts (A)(B)(C)(D); name the faulty one, or "no error" |
| **Sentence correction** | a phrase is underlined; pick the best replacement |
| **Sentence improvement** | same as correction, with "no improvement" as an option |

---

## The scan order

Do **not** read the sentence for meaning first. Run this checklist in order — the error is almost always in the first two steps.

**1. Find the subject and the main verb. Check agreement.**  
   Delete every prepositional phrase first. This alone catches roughly a third of all errors.

**2. Check the tense.** Is it consistent? Do the time markers (since, ago, by the time) match the tense used?

**3. Check prepositions.** Scan for the fixed pairs — *senior to*, *different from*,  
   *discuss* (no *about*), *comprises* (no *of*), *married to*, *prefer to*.

**4. Check pronouns.** Number agreement, and case after *than* / *as*.

**5. Check articles.** a/an by sound; missing or extra *the*.

**6. Check parallelism** in any list, and any *not only… but also* pair.

**7. Check comparisons.** Are two like things being compared? Any double comparative?

**8. Only now, read for sense.** Misplaced modifiers, redundancy, wrong word.

> If you reach step 8 without finding anything, seriously consider **"no error"**. It is a
> real option and is chosen far less often than it is correct.

## Worked examples

### Example 1

*(A) The list of candidates (B) who have applied (C) for the post (D) were displayed yesterday.*

- Step 1: subject is **list** (singular). Delete "of candidates who have applied for the
  post" → "The list … were displayed".
- **Error in (D)** — should be **was displayed**.

### Example 2

*(A) He is one of (B) the best player (C) that our college (D) has ever produced.*

- Step 1: "one of the" must be followed by a **plural** noun.
- **Error in (B)** — should be **the best players**.

### Example 3

*(A) Ram is senior (B) than me (C) by three years (D) in this office.*

- Step 3: *senior* takes **to**.
- **Error in (B)** — should be **senior to me**.

### Example 4

*(A) Neither the manager (B) nor his assistants (C) was present (D) at the meeting.*

- Step 1: neither/nor agrees with the **nearer** subject, which is *assistants* (plural).
- **Error in (C)** — should be **were present**.

### Example 5

*(A) The climate of Chennai (B) is hotter (C) than Bangalore (D) in summer.*

- Step 7: a climate is being compared to a city.
- **Error in (C)** — should be **than that of Bangalore**.

### Example 6

*(A) Having finished the assignment, (B) the television was switched on (C) by the students (D) to relax.*

- Steps 1–7 pass. Step 8: who finished the assignment? Grammatically, the television did.
- **Error in (B)** — should be **the students switched on the television**.

### Example 7

*(A) She has been working (B) in this company (C) since five years (D) and enjoys it.*

- Step 2: **since** takes a point in time; **for** takes a duration.
- **Error in (C)** — should be **for five years**.

## Sentence correction: choosing between options

When you must pick a replacement rather than just name the error:

1. **Identify the error first**, before reading the options. Otherwise the options will
   confuse you.
2. **Eliminate options that repeat the original error.** Usually two of four do.
3. Of the survivors, prefer the one that is **shorter and in the active voice**, provided it
   is grammatical and does not change the meaning.
4. Check that the replacement fits the rest of the sentence — options often fix one error and
   introduce another.

> **Never pick an option because it "sounds more impressive".** Exam answers are plain. If
> two options are both grammatical, the simpler one is the intended answer.

## The redundancy list

Sentence improvement questions love these. Each phrase says the same thing twice:

| Redundant | Correct |
|---|---|
| return back | return |
| repeat again | repeat |
| revert back | revert |
| ascend up / descend down | ascend / descend |
| join together | join |
| new innovation | innovation |
| free gift | gift |
| final conclusion | conclusion |
| past history | history |
| advance planning | planning |
| each and every | each *or* every |
| cancel out | cancel |
| enter into (a room) | enter |
| discuss about | discuss |
| more preferable | preferable |

## Practice set

Each sentence is split into parts. Name the faulty part, or answer "no error".

**Q1.** (A) One of my friend (B) is going to (C) attend the conference (D) next week.  
→ **(A)** — "one of my **friends**" needs a plural noun.

**Q2.** (A) The list of items (B) that were discussed (C) in the meeting (D) are attached.  
→ **(D)** — the subject is *list*, so **is attached**.

**Q3.** (A) He has been (B) working in this company (C) since ten years (D) now.  
→ **(C)** — a duration takes **for ten years**.

**Q4.** (A) Each of the students (B) have submitted (C) their assignment (D) on time.  
→ **(B)** — "each of" is singular, so **has submitted**.

**Q5.** (A) The population of India (B) is greater (C) than China (D) by a large margin.  
→ **(C)** — compare like with like: **than that of China**.

**Q6.** (A) Ram is more (B) intelligent than (C) any boy (D) in his class.  
→ **(C)** — Ram is himself in the class, so it must be **any other boy**.

**Q7.** (A) I prefer coffee (B) than tea (C) in the morning (D) no error.  
→ **(B)** — *prefer X* **to** *Y*.

**Q8.** (A) Not only he (B) insulted the guest (C) but also (D) refused to apologise.  
→ **(A)** — parallelism requires **He not only insulted**.

**Q9.** (A) The teacher along with (B) the students (C) were present (D) in the hall.  
→ **(C)** — "along with" keeps the subject singular: **was present**.

**Q10.** (A) He returned back (B) from Delhi (C) yesterday evening (D) no error.  
→ **(A)** — *returned back* is redundant; just **returned**.

**Q11.** (A) Neither of the two boys (B) were able (C) to solve (D) the problem.  
→ **(B)** — *neither* is singular: **was able**.

**Q12.** (A) The committee have (B) decided to (C) postpone the (D) annual function.  
→ **(A)** — collective noun acting as one body: **has decided**.

**Q13.** (A) He is one of (B) the most brilliant student (C) that this college (D) has  
produced.  
→ **(B)** — **students**, plural.

**Q14.** (A) I have been knowing (B) him (C) for the last (D) five years.  
→ **(A)** — *know* is a stative verb and takes no continuous form: **have known**.

**Q15.** (A) Hardly had he (B) reached the station (C) than the train (D) departed.  
→ **(C)** — *hardly … when*, not *than*.

**Q16.** (A) The scissors (B) is lying (C) on the table (D) in the kitchen.  
→ **(B)** — *scissors* is plural: **are lying**.

**Q17.** (A) She said that (B) she will come (C) to the party (D) tomorrow.  
→ **(B)** — reported speech shifts back: **she would come**.

**Q18.** (A) Both the brothers (B) as well as their father (C) are working (D) in the same  
firm.  
→ **(C)** — "as well as" leaves the subject as *both the brothers*, which is plural, so  
**are working** is correct. **No error.**  
*This is deliberately placed after Q9 — check whether the true subject is singular or plural before assuming the phrase changes it.*

**Q19.** (A) The reason why (B) he failed is (C) because he (D) did not prepare.  
→ **(C)** — "the reason … is because" is redundant: **is that**.

**Q20.** (A) He has no (B) other alternative (C) but to (D) accept the offer.  
→ **(B)** — *alternative* already implies another option: **no alternative**.

### Sentence improvement

#### Q21. "If I would have known, I would have helped."

Conditional type 3 uses the past perfect in the *if* clause.  
→ **"If I had known, I would have helped."**

#### Q22. "The furnitures in the room are new."

*Furniture* is uncountable.  
→ **"The furniture in the room is new."**

#### Q23. "He is married with a doctor."

→ **"He is married to a doctor."**

#### Q24. "Despite of his illness, he attended."

*Despite* takes no *of*; *in spite of* does.  
→ **"Despite his illness, he attended."** *(or "In spite of his illness")*

#### Q25. "Please revert back to me at the earliest."

*Revert* already means "come back".  
→ **"Please revert to me at the earliest."**

## Traps

- Scanning for "what sounds odd" instead of running the checklist. Your ear is unreliable on
  exactly the constructions that get tested.
- Marking the part that *contains* the subject when the error is in the part that contains
  the **verb** — the error is where the wrong word is.
- Overlooking "no error". Roughly one question in six has none.
- Fixing the error but choosing an option that changes the sentence's meaning.
- Assuming a long, formal-sounding option is better.

## Checkpoints

- [ ] I run the scan order rather than reading for meaning first
- [ ] I delete prepositional phrases before checking the verb
- [ ] I check tense markers (since/for/ago/by the time) against the tense used
- [ ] I scan for the fixed-preposition errors on every question
- [ ] I check comparisons for like-with-like
- [ ] I look for dangling modifiers only after the mechanical checks
- [ ] I am willing to answer "no error" when the checklist comes up clean
- [ ] I identify the error before reading the answer options
- [ ] I eliminate options that repeat the original error
- [ ] I know the redundancy list and can spot "return back" style pairs instantly
