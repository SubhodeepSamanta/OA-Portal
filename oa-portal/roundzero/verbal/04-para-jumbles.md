# Para jumbles

Four to six sentences in the wrong order; put them right. Accenture asks 2 per paper, and they appear in TCS and Infosys too. There is a reliable procedure — most people guess instead.

## The method

**Step 1 — find the opening sentence.**

An opener is **independent**: it makes sense on its own and introduces the subject with a full noun.

| An opener **has** | An opener **never has** |
|---|---|
| a full noun phrase ("The Indian textile industry…") | a pronoun with no antecedent (it, this, they, he) |
| a general or introductory statement | a connector (however, therefore, moreover, but, also) |
| the topic being introduced for the first time | a definite reference ("the aforementioned scheme") |
| | a conclusion marker (thus, in conclusion, finally) |

**Step 2 — find mandatory pairs.**

Two sentences that must be adjacent. Look for:

- **Noun → pronoun.** A sentence naming *Dr. Sharma* must precede one saying *he*.
- **Full form → abbreviation.** "the World Trade Organization (WTO)" comes before "the WTO".
- **Question → answer.**
- **Cause → effect**, marked by *therefore*, *as a result*, *consequently*.
- **General → specific**, marked by *for example*, *for instance*, *such as*.
- **Contrast**, marked by *however*, *but*, *on the other hand* — the contradicted statement
  comes first.
- **Chronology.** Dates, "first/then/later/finally", tense shifts.

> Finding **one** mandatory pair usually eliminates two of the four options immediately. This
> is the highest-value step, and it is faster than reconstructing the whole paragraph.

**Step 3 — find the closing sentence.**

Look for *thus*, *therefore*, *in conclusion*, *finally*, a summary, or a recommendation.

**Step 4 — use the options.**

This is a multiple-choice question. **Do not build the paragraph from scratch.** Check which options begin with your chosen opener, then test your mandatory pair against the survivors.  
Usually only one remains.

**Step 5 — read your answer through once.** It should flow. If a transition jars, you have it wrong.

## Worked example

*Arrange A, B, C, D:*

- **A.** However, this convenience has come at a significant cost to local retailers.
- **B.** Online shopping has transformed the way Indians buy everyday goods.
- **C.** Many small shopkeepers report a sharp fall in footfall since 2020.
- **D.** It allows consumers to compare prices and order from home within minutes.

**Step 1 — opener.** B introduces the topic with a full noun phrase and no connector. A starts with *However*, D starts with *It*, and C refers to a fall that needs prior context.  
→ **B is first.**

**Step 2 — pairs.**
- D's *It* refers to online shopping → **B–D**.
- A's *this convenience* refers to what D describes → **D–A**.
- C gives evidence for A's "cost to local retailers" → **A–C**.

**Result: B – D – A – C.**

**Step 5 — read it through:** Online shopping has transformed buying. It allows comparison and home delivery. However, this convenience has cost local retailers. Many shopkeepers report falling footfall. ✓ Flows cleanly.

## Worked example — using the options

*Arrange A, B, C, D. Options: (i) BDAC (ii) DBCA (iii) BCDA (iv) CABD*

Suppose you are confident only that **A must follow D** (A begins "As a result of this policy…", D describes the policy).

- (i) B**DA**C → D then A ✓
- (ii) D**B**CA → D then B ✗
- (iii) BC**DA** → D then A ✓
- (iv) CAB**D** → D is last, A is not after it ✗

Two options survive from one pair. Now separate them: (i) opens with B and puts C last;  
(iii) opens with B and puts C second. Check whether C can follow B directly — if C contains a pronoun referring to something only D introduces, then (iii) fails and **(i)** is the answer.

That is the whole technique: **partial certainty plus option elimination**, not full reconstruction.

## The signal words, sorted

| Signal | Position |
|---|---|
| Full noun phrase, general statement | **opener** |
| it, this, that, they, he, she, such, these | **never** the opener |
| however, but, although, on the contrary, nevertheless | after the idea being contradicted |
| moreover, furthermore, in addition, also, besides | after a related point |
| therefore, thus, hence, consequently, as a result | after the cause |
| for example, for instance, such as | after the general statement |
| first, then, next, later, finally | chronological order |
| in conclusion, to sum up, overall | **closer** |

## Practice set

#### Q1. Arrange A, B, C, D.

- **A.** These devices now monitor everything from heart rate to sleep quality.
- **B.** Wearable technology has moved rapidly from novelty to necessity.
- **C.** However, questions about data privacy remain largely unanswered.
- **D.** Manufacturers collect vast quantities of personal health information.

**Opener:** B — full noun phrase, no connector, introduces the topic. A begins with "These devices" (needs an antecedent), C begins with "However", D presupposes context.  
**Pairs:** B → A ("These devices" = wearables). C raises privacy; D explains why privacy is a concern, so C → D.

**Answer: B – A – C – D**

#### Q2. Arrange A, B, C, D.

- **A.** It was designed to make banking accessible to rural households.
- **B.** The scheme has since opened more than 400 million accounts.
- **C.** The Jan Dhan Yojana was launched in 2014.
- **D.** Many of these accounts, however, remain dormant.

**Opener:** C — names the subject in full and gives the date.  
**Pairs:** C → A ("It" = the scheme). B gives the outcome. D qualifies B with "these accounts".

**Answer: C – A – B – D**

#### Q3. Arrange A, B, C, D, E.

- **A.** This has led several states to invest in rainwater harvesting.
- **B.** Groundwater levels in northern India have fallen sharply over two decades.
- **C.** Agriculture accounts for the overwhelming share of this extraction.
- **D.** The results, though promising, are still limited in scale.
- **E.** Farmers rely on borewells because canal supply is unreliable.

**Opener:** B — states the problem with a full noun phrase.  
**Chain:** B states the fall → C attributes it to agriculture → E explains why farmers extract → A is the response ("This has led") → D evaluates the response.

**Answer: B – C – E – A – D**

#### Q4. Using the options to eliminate.

- **A.** As a result, several of them have shut down.
- **B.** Small bookstores face intense competition from online retailers.
- **C.** Others have survived by becoming community spaces.
- **D.** They host readings, workshops and book clubs.

*Options:* (i) BACD (ii) BADC (iii) BCAD (iv) BDAC

All four options open with **B**, so the opener does not discriminate — go to pairs.
- "As a result" (A) must follow the cause, which is B. → A immediately after B.
  That leaves **(i) BACD** and **(ii) BADC**.
- C says "Others have survived", contrasting with A's "several have shut down" — so C follows
  A. That fits (i). In (ii), D would follow A, but D's "They host readings" needs C's  
  "community spaces" first.

**Answer: (i) B – A – C – D**

> Q4 shows the real technique: when the opener is common to all options, **the mandatory pair
> does all the work.**

#### Q5. Arrange A, B, C, D.

- **A.** Consequently, many graduates find themselves underemployed.
- **B.** India produces over a million engineering graduates each year.
- **C.** Industry surveys repeatedly report a shortage of employable skills.
- **D.** The gap lies not in the number of graduates but in their training.

**Opener:** B — a bare fact with a full noun phrase.  
**Chain:** B (many graduates) → C (yet skills are short) → A ("Consequently" — the result) →  
D (the diagnosis, using "The gap").

**Answer: B – C – A – D**

## Traps

- Starting to reconstruct from scratch instead of testing the options.
- Ignoring pronouns. A sentence beginning with *It* or *They* is almost never first.
- Assuming the sentences are in a logical order when they are actually in a **chronological**
  one — check for dates and tense shifts.
- Two sentences both look like openers: pick the more **general** one. Paragraphs go from
  general to specific.
- Spending three minutes. Budget 90 seconds; if you have a mandatory pair and two surviving
  options, take the better one and move on.

## Checkpoints

- [ ] I identify the opener by looking for a full noun phrase and no connector
- [ ] I know a sentence starting with a pronoun or a connector is never first
- [ ] I look for at least one mandatory pair before touching the options
- [ ] I use noun→pronoun and full-form→abbreviation to fix pair order
- [ ] I identify the closer by its summary or conclusion marker
- [ ] I eliminate options using my mandatory pair rather than rebuilding the paragraph
- [ ] I read my chosen order through once to check the transitions
- [ ] I choose the more general sentence when two look like openers
- [ ] I keep each jumble under 90 seconds
