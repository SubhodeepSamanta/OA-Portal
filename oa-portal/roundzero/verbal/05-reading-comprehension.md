# Reading comprehension

Accenture asks 3 RC questions; TCS's updated pattern uses **passage recall**. RC is where verbal sections are won or lost on **time**, not on vocabulary.

## The strategic decision

RC takes 4–6 minutes for 3–5 marks. In a sectionally timed paper that is a large share of your budget.

> **Do RC last.** Clear the sentence completions, error spotting and jumbles first — they are
> 20–30 seconds each. Then give RC whatever remains. If you run out of time, you will have
> lost 3 marks instead of 12.

---

## The method

**1. Skim the questions first — but only the question stems, not the options.**  
Ten seconds. You are looking for what to watch for: a date, a name, a cause, the author's opinion. Reading the options first biases your reading and costs more time than it saves.

**2. Read the passage once, at a steady pace, without re-reading sentences.**  
Do not stop at unfamiliar words — context usually carries them, and you rarely need them.

**3. As you read, note the structure**, mentally or in the margin:

- What is the **main point**? (usually in the first or last paragraph)
- What is the **author's attitude**? (approving, critical, neutral, cautious)
- Where do the **shifts** happen? (however, but, on the other hand)

**4. Answer, returning to the passage for every factual question.**  
Never answer a detail question from memory. Locate the line.

---

## The five question types

| Type | How to handle it |
|---|---|
| **Main idea / title** | must cover the *whole* passage, not one paragraph |
| **Detail / factual** | find the exact line; the answer is a paraphrase of it |
| **Inference** | must follow from the text, but is not stated in it |
| **Vocabulary in context** | substitute each option into the sentence; ignore the dictionary meaning |
| **Tone / attitude** | look at adjectives and adverbs the author chooses |

### Main idea

The right answer is **neither too narrow nor too broad**.

- Too narrow: covers only one paragraph → wrong
- Too broad: covers the general subject but not this passage's argument → wrong
- Right: states what the passage as a whole is saying

### Detail

The correct option is a **paraphrase**, not a quotation. An option that repeats the passage's exact words is often a distractor with one word changed.

### Inference

The trap here is going too far. An inference must be **necessary**, not merely plausible.  
Ask: *could the passage be true and this be false?* If yes, it is not the answer.

### Vocabulary in context

The tested word usually has several meanings, and the passage uses a secondary one. Put each option back into the original sentence and read it aloud in your head. The one that preserves the sentence's meaning is correct — even if it is not the word's commonest sense.

### Tone

Common answers, roughly from positive to negative:

*appreciative, optimistic, objective, neutral, analytical, cautious, sceptical, critical, pessimistic, sarcastic*

> **Most exam passages are "objective", "analytical" or "critical".** Extreme options —
> *sarcastic*, *contemptuous*, *euphoric* — are rarely correct. Academic prose is measured.

---

## How wrong options are built

Recognising these saves more time than any reading technique:

| Distractor type | How to spot it |
|---|---|
| **Extreme language** | contains *all, never, always, only, must, impossible* |
| **Half right** | the first half matches the passage, the second half does not |
| **Out of scope** | true and sensible, but not discussed in the passage |
| **Reversed** | states the opposite of the passage, often subtly |
| **Right answer, wrong question** | a true statement that does not answer what was asked |

> **Eliminate on the extreme-language rule first.** In a typical RC set it removes one option
> per question at zero cost.

## Worked example

*Passage extract:*

> "Microfinance was initially hailed as a decisive tool against rural poverty. Early studies
> reported significant gains in household income among borrowers. More recent randomised
> evaluations, however, have found far more modest effects, and in some regions no
> measurable improvement in consumption at all. This does not mean microfinance is without
> value — it has clearly widened access to formal credit — but the early claims now appear
> to have been overstated."

**Q: What is the author's attitude towards microfinance?**

- (a) Wholly dismissive → ✗ the passage says it "has clearly widened access"
- (b) Uncritically enthusiastic → ✗ the passage questions the early claims
- (c) Measured, acknowledging benefits while questioning early claims → ✓
- (d) Indifferent → ✗ the author engages closely with the evidence

**Answer: (c).** Note how (a) and (b) are both *extremes* — that pattern recurs constantly.

**Q: Which of the following can be inferred?**

- (a) Microfinance should be discontinued → too strong; not supported
- (b) Early studies of microfinance may have had methodological limitations → ✓ implied by
  the contrast with "more recent randomised evaluations"
- (c) Randomised evaluations are always more reliable → "always" is extreme
- (d) Rural poverty has increased → out of scope

**Answer: (b).**

## TCS passage recall

TCS's format shows a passage, then removes it and asks factual questions from memory.

- **Read for facts, not for argument.** Names, numbers, dates, sequences.
- Make a very short mental list of the 3–4 concrete details as you read.
- The questions are factual, not inferential — so retention beats analysis here.

## Practice set

### Passage 1

> India's transition to electric vehicles has been slower than policymakers hoped. Although
> subsidies have reduced purchase prices substantially, charging infrastructure remains
> concentrated in a handful of metropolitan areas. Buyers outside these cities face what
> researchers call "range anxiety" — the fear of being stranded without a charging point.
> Manufacturers argue that they cannot justify large production runs until demand rises,
> while consumers say they will not buy until the charging network expands. The result is a
> familiar deadlock. Some states have attempted to break it by mandating charging points at
> fuel stations, an approach that has shown early promise but has not yet been evaluated
> rigorously.

#### Q1. The main idea of the passage is:

(a) Electric vehicles are too expensive for Indian buyers (b) A mutual dependency between demand and infrastructure is slowing EV adoption (c) State governments have solved the EV charging problem (d) Range anxiety is an irrational fear

→ **(b).** (a) is contradicted — subsidies cut prices. (c) overstates "early promise".  
(d) inserts a judgement the passage never makes.

#### Q2. "The result is a familiar deadlock" refers to:

(a) the conflict between state and central government (b) manufacturers waiting for demand while consumers wait for infrastructure (c) the shortage of subsidies (d) disagreement among researchers

→ **(b)** — the sentence immediately before it describes exactly this standoff.

#### Q3. The author's attitude towards the state-level mandate is:

(a) enthusiastic (b) dismissive (c) cautiously positive (d) hostile

→ **(c)** — "shown early promise" is positive, "not yet been evaluated rigorously" is the caution. (a) is too strong.

#### Q4. Which can be inferred?

(a) Subsidies have failed entirely (b) Charging infrastructure is unevenly distributed across India (c) Manufacturers are indifferent to consumer demand (d) EVs will never succeed in India

→ **(b)** — "concentrated in a handful of metropolitan areas" implies uneven distribution.  
(a), (c), (d) are all extreme.

### Passage 2

> The rise of standardised testing in recruitment reflects a practical problem: firms
> receive far more applications than they can meaningfully review. A test offers a defensible,
> uniform filter. Critics counter that such tests measure a narrow band of ability and
> systematically disadvantage candidates from weaker schooling backgrounds, who may have the
> aptitude but not the test-taking practice. Defenders reply that the alternative — relying on
> the reputation of a candidate's college — is more biased still, since college admission is
> itself shaped by family circumstances. The debate is unlikely to be settled soon, in part
> because both sides are describing real effects.

#### Q5. The primary purpose of the passage is to:

(a) argue that standardised tests should be abolished (b) present a balanced account of a genuine disagreement (c) defend recruiters against criticism (d) explain how to prepare for aptitude tests

→ **(b)** — the final sentence explicitly says both sides describe real effects.

#### Q6. According to the passage, defenders of testing argue that:

(a) tests are perfectly fair (b) tests measure every relevant ability (c) the alternative of college reputation is even more biased (d) schooling background does not matter

→ **(c)** — stated directly. (a) and (b) are stronger than anything the passage claims.

#### Q7. The word "defensible" in the first paragraph most nearly means:

(a) protective (b) justifiable (c) aggressive (d) fortified

→ **(b).** *Defensible* has a military sense too, but here it describes a filter a firm can justify — substitute each option back into the sentence to confirm.

#### Q8. Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the critics' position?

(a) Test scores correlate strongly with later job performance across all backgrounds (b) Many firms use tests (c) Tests are cheap to administer (d) Some candidates enjoy taking tests

→ **(a).** The critics claim tests measure a narrow band of ability; evidence that scores predict actual performance regardless of background attacks that claim directly. (b), (c) and (d) are irrelevant to whether the test measures the right thing.

> **Q8 is a "strengthen/weaken" question** — increasingly common. Ask precisely *which claim*
> is being attacked, then find the option that bears on that claim and no other.

## Traps

- Answering detail questions from memory instead of locating the line.
- Choosing an option because it is true in the real world. Only the passage counts.
- Selecting a too-narrow option for a main-idea question because it is accurate.
- Reading the options before the passage, which biases your reading.
- Spending eight minutes on one passage and abandoning the rest of the section.
- Getting stuck on an unfamiliar word. Skip it and continue.
