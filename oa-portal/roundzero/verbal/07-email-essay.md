# Email & essay writing

**TCS** now includes a professional **email-writing** task. **Wipro** scores a 20-minute  
**essay**. Both are marked largely on structure, grammar and length — which means both are almost entirely preparable. Most candidates improvise and lose easy marks.

---

# Part 1 — The TCS email task

You are given a scenario and a set of **keywords or phrases that must all appear**.

## The marking reality

You are scored on:

1. **Using every given keyword** (the biggest single factor — omitting one costs
   disproportionately)
2. **Correct email structure** (salutation, body, closing)
3. **Grammar and spelling**
4. **Appropriate formal tone**
5. **Length** — typically 50–120 words

You are **not** scored on originality. Do not try to be interesting.

## The template

```
Dear Sir / Madam,          ←  or "Dear Mr. Sharma," if a name is given

[Opening line: why you are writing — 1 sentence]

[Body: the details, working in every keyword — 2 to 4 sentences]

[Closing line: what you want to happen next — 1 sentence]

Regards,
[Your name]
```

## Worked example

**Scenario:** *Write an email to your manager requesting leave.*  
**Keywords:** *urgent, family, three days, handover, apologise, resume*

> Dear Sir,
>
> I am writing to request leave for **three days**, from 12 to 14 September, due to an
> **urgent family** matter that requires my presence.
>
> I have completed a **handover** of my current tasks to Rahul, who has agreed to cover my
> responsibilities during this period. I **apologise** for the short notice and have ensured
> that no deliverable will be affected.
>
> I will **resume** work on 15 September. Please let me know if you need any further
> information.
>
> Regards,
> Subhodeep

Every keyword appears, in a natural sentence, in a correct structure. That is a full-marks answer.

## Rules

- **Use every keyword.** Tick them off as you write. This is the commonest way marks are lost.
- **Keep sentences short.** Short sentences are harder to get wrong.
- **Formal register:** no contractions (*I am*, not *I'm*), no slang, no exclamation marks.
- **Never leave it blank.** A short, correct email beats nothing by a wide margin.
- Watch the **word limit** — going far over is penalised as much as going under.

## Useful formal phrasings

| Purpose | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Opening | I am writing to inform you that… / I would like to request… |
| Apologising | I apologise for any inconvenience caused. |
| Requesting | I would be grateful if you could… |
| Attaching | Please find attached… |
| Closing | Please let me know if you require any further information. |
| Sign-off | Regards, / Sincerely, / Best regards, |

# Part 2 — The Wipro essay (20 minutes)

You get one prompt and 20 minutes. Typical topics: technology's impact, work–life balance, social media, online education, environmental responsibility, AI and jobs.

## The marking reality

Graded on **structure, grammar, coherence and length** — not on the brilliance of your argument. A clear, balanced, well-organised essay with simple correct English scores higher than an ambitious one with errors.

## The time split

| Minutes | Do this |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | Plan: pick a side or take a balanced view; jot 3 points |
| 3–17 | Write |
| 17–20 | Proofread for grammar, spelling and repeated words |

**Do not skip the plan and do not skip the proofread.** Three minutes of planning prevents the rambling middle paragraph that costs the most marks.

## The structure — five paragraphs

**1. Introduction (3–4 sentences).** Restate the topic in your own words, then state your position clearly. End with a sentence signposting what the essay will cover.

**2, 3, 4. Body paragraphs (4–5 sentences each).** One idea per paragraph:
- open with a topic sentence stating the point
- explain it
- give an example
- link back to the question

**5. Conclusion (3–4 sentences).** Summarise the points, restate the position in different words, and end with a forward-looking line. **Introduce nothing new here.**

Target **250–350 words** unless told otherwise.

## Linking words — use these deliberately

| Function | Words |
|---|---|
| Adding | Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, Additionally |
| Contrasting | However, On the other hand, Nevertheless, Conversely |
| Giving reasons | Because, Since, Owing to, As a result of |
| Giving results | Therefore, Consequently, Hence, Thus |
| Exemplifying | For instance, For example, To illustrate |
| Concluding | In conclusion, To sum up, Overall |

**One linking word per paragraph opening** is enough. Stacking them makes the writing worse.

## A skeleton you can reuse for any topic

> **Intro:** *The question of whether [X] is increasingly debated. While [X] offers clear
> benefits in terms of [A], it also raises serious concerns about [B]. This essay argues
> that [position].*
>
> **Body 1:** *The most significant advantage of [X] is [A]. …For instance, … This suggests
> that …*
>
> **Body 2:** *However, [X] also presents real difficulties. …*
>
> **Body 3:** *A balanced approach is therefore necessary. …*
>
> **Conclusion:** *In conclusion, [X] is neither wholly beneficial nor wholly harmful.
> …Managed carefully, it can [positive outcome].*

Fill the brackets and you have a competent essay on any of the standard prompts. Practise writing two of these to time before the drive.

## Practice set

### Email 1 — complaint

**Scenario:** Write to a supplier about a delayed order.  
**Keywords:** *purchase order, delay, inconvenience, revised timeline, escalate*

> Dear Sir/Madam,
>
> I am writing regarding **purchase order** PO-4471, placed on 3 September, which was due for
> delivery on 20 September. We have not yet received the consignment, and this **delay** has
> caused considerable **inconvenience** to our production schedule.
>
> I would be grateful if you could confirm a **revised timeline** for delivery by the end of
> this week. If we do not receive a response, we will have to **escalate** the matter to your
> regional office.
>
> Please let me know if you require any further details.
>
> Regards,
> Subhodeep Samanta

### Email 2 — request to a professor

**Scenario:** Request a recommendation letter.  
**Keywords:** *internship, deadline, transcript, grateful, attached*

> Dear Professor Sharma,
>
> I am applying for a summer **internship** at Infosys and would like to request a letter of
> recommendation from you. The application **deadline** is 15 October.
>
> I have **attached** my updated resume and academic **transcript** for your reference. I
> would be **grateful** if you could let me know whether you are able to write on my behalf.
>
> Thank you for your time.
>
> Regards,
> Subhodeep Samanta

### Email 3 — apology

**Scenario:** Apologise to a client for a missed meeting.  
**Keywords:** *sincerely apologise, unavoidable, reschedule, convenience, assure*

> Dear Mr. Verma,
>
> I **sincerely apologise** for missing our scheduled meeting on Tuesday. An **unavoidable**
> medical emergency prevented me from attending, and I regret that I could not inform you in
> advance.
>
> I would like to **reschedule** at your earliest **convenience** and am available any day
> this week. I **assure** you that this will not happen again.
>
> Thank you for your understanding.
>
> Regards,
> Subhodeep Samanta

> **Note what these three have in common:** identical structure, short sentences, every
> keyword used naturally, and no attempt to be interesting. That is a full-marks answer.

### Essay — a worked example

**Prompt:** *Is social media doing more harm than good?* (20 minutes)

**Plan (3 minutes):** Balanced view. Points: (1) connection and access to information, (2) misinformation and mental health, (3) regulation and digital literacy as the answer.

**Introduction**

> Few technologies have divided opinion as sharply as social media. In less than two decades
> it has become the primary means by which billions of people communicate, learn and organise.
> While its benefits in connectivity and access to information are undeniable, the harms it
> causes to mental health and public discourse are equally real. This essay argues that
> social media is neither inherently good nor bad, and that the outcome depends largely on how
> it is regulated and used.

**Body 1 — the case for**

> The most significant advantage of social media is the democratisation of information.
> Ordinary citizens can now publish, organise and hold institutions to account without the
> permission of any editor or broadcaster. For instance, small businesses in Indian towns now
> reach national markets through platforms that cost them nothing to join. This access simply
> did not exist a generation ago.

**Body 2 — the case against**

> However, the same openness allows misinformation to spread faster than corrections can
> follow. Furthermore, research increasingly links heavy social media use among adolescents
> to anxiety and disrupted sleep. The design of these platforms rewards engagement rather
> than accuracy, which means the most provocative content, not the most reliable, tends to
> travel furthest.

**Body 3 — the resolution**

> A balanced approach is therefore necessary. Rather than banning platforms outright, which
> is neither practical nor desirable, governments and schools should focus on transparency in
> algorithms and on teaching digital literacy from an early age. Users who understand how
> their attention is being monetised make better decisions about how they spend it.

**Conclusion**

> In conclusion, social media is a tool whose effects depend on the framework surrounding it.
> Its capacity to connect and inform is genuine, as is its capacity to mislead and isolate.
> Managed with sensible regulation and an informed public, it can remain a net benefit rather
> than a net cost.

**Word count: ~320.** Five paragraphs, one idea each, one linking word per opening, no new material in the conclusion.

## Traps

- **Omitting a keyword** in the email task. Tick them off.
- Informal tone: contractions, "Hi", exclamation marks, emoji-style punctuation.
- Writing far more than the limit because you know the topic well.
- No paragraph breaks. A wall of text loses structural marks by itself.
- Taking an extreme position that you cannot support in three paragraphs. Balanced is safer
  and easier.
- Skipping the proofread. Two spelling corrections are worth more than one extra sentence.
- Leaving it blank because you ran out of time — write a short answer instead.
