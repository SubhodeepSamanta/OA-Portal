# Communication assessment

**Accenture scores 20 questions in 20 minutes. Cognizant runs one for GenC.** These are automated spoken-English tests — usually Versant or SHL — graded by software, not a human.

That matters enormously, because **software grades differently from a person**. It scores mechanics: fluency, pausing, pronunciation clarity, sentence construction. It does not care whether you are interesting, and it cannot be charmed.

---

## What the machine is actually measuring

| Score component | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Sentence mastery** | grammar and sentence construction |
| **Vocabulary** | range and appropriateness of words |
| **Fluency** | rhythm and pace — **long pauses hurt badly** |
| **Pronunciation** | clarity of individual sounds |

> **Fluency is where most Indian candidates lose marks, and it is not about accent.** The
> algorithm penalises hesitation, restarts and filler ("umm", "actually", "like"). A simple
> sentence delivered smoothly scores higher than a complex one delivered in fragments.

---

## The task types

### 1. Read aloud

Sentences appear on screen; you read them.

- **Read at a steady, moderate pace** — not fast.
- Read the sentence silently first if given a moment.
- If you stumble, **keep going**. Do not restart — a restart costs more than the error.

### 2. Repeat the sentence

You hear a sentence and repeat it exactly. Length increases as you go.

- This tests working memory as much as speech.
- **Repeat the rhythm, not just the words.** The system is comparing your prosody.
- If you miss a word, say the rest confidently rather than stopping.

### 3. Jumbled / sentence build

You hear or see fragments and must speak them as a correct sentence.

- Find the **verb** first, then the subject, then the rest.
- Say it once, cleanly. Do not think aloud.

### 4. Short answer questions

A simple factual question: *"What do you use to write on paper — a pen or a plate?"*

- These are deliberately easy; they test comprehension speed.
- Answer in **one or two words**. Do not elaborate.

### 5. Story retell

You hear a short story and must retell it in your own words.

- Capture **who, what, where, and the outcome**.
- Use your own words — echoing verbatim scores lower than paraphrasing.
- Keep talking for the full time allowed. Silence is the worst outcome.

### 6. Open question

An opinion question with 40–60 seconds to answer.

- **Structure it:** state your position → give two reasons → conclude.
- Fill the whole time. Do not finish in 15 seconds.

## The habits that raise the score

**Speak in complete sentences.** "Yes, I agree with that because…" rather than "Yeah, true."

**Slow down by about 15%.** Most candidates speak too fast under stress, which blurs consonants and triggers pronunciation penalties.

**Eliminate fillers.** "Umm", "aa", "basically", "actually", "you know", "like". Silence for half a second is better than a filler — but a smooth sentence is better than both.

**Finish your sentences.** Trailing off mid-sentence is heavily penalised. Even if the thought is imperfect, complete the grammar.

**Mind the sounds Indian speakers are commonly marked down on:**

| Sound | Watch for |
|---|---|
| **v / w** | *vine* vs *wine* — keep them distinct |
| **th** | *think*, *three* — not *tink* or *tree* |
| **s / sh** | *sip* vs *ship* |
| **Final consonants** | say the *d* in *asked*, the *t* in *helped* |
| **Word stress** | de-VE-lop, not DE-ve-lop · PHO-to-graph vs pho-TO-gra-pher |

**Test your setup before you start.** A poor microphone or background noise directly lowers a machine-graded score. Use a quiet room and a headset if you have one.

---

## Practice routine

Ten minutes daily for two weeks moves the score noticeably.

1. **Read a news article aloud** for 3 minutes, recording yourself.
2. **Play it back.** Count the fillers and the restarts. That number is your target to reduce.
3. **Answer one opinion question** for 60 seconds without stopping, on any topic.
4. **Repeat-after-audio:** play any English clip, pause, repeat the sentence exactly.

Recording and listening back is the part that works. Reading aloud without hearing yourself does very little.

## Practice set

#### Q1. You mispronounce a word halfway through a read-aloud task. What do you do?

**Keep going.** A restart costs more fluency marks than the single error does.

#### Q2. Why do fillers like "umm" and "basically" matter so much?

The scoring is largely fluency-based, and fillers are counted as disfluencies. They lower the score directly, regardless of how correct the content is.

#### Q3. In the short-answer task, should you give a full sentence?

No — one or two words. The task measures comprehension speed, and elaborating wastes the window.

#### Q4. In the open question you finish your point in 15 seconds of a 60-second window. Is that good?

No. Fill the time. Unused time is scored as reduced fluency and limited range.

#### Q5. In the story retell, is it better to echo the exact wording or paraphrase?

**Paraphrase.** Retelling in your own words demonstrates vocabulary and comprehension;  
verbatim echoing does not.

#### Q6. Name three sounds Indian English speakers are most often penalised on.

**v/w**, **th**, and dropped **final consonants**. Word stress placement is a fourth.

#### Q7. Should you speak faster to sound fluent?

No — the opposite. Slow down about 15%. Speed blurs consonants and triggers pronunciation penalties; smoothness, not speed, is what fluency measures.

## Traps

- Treating it like a human conversation. Charm, humour and personality score nothing.
- Restarting sentences.
- Speaking too fast under nerves.
- Trailing off without completing the grammar.
- Long silences while you think of a better word — say the simpler word.
- A noisy room or bad microphone dragging down an otherwise good performance.
- Preparing content instead of practising delivery. The test grades **how** you speak.
