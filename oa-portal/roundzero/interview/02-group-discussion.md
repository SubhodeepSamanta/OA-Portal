# Group discussion

Used as an elimination round by several volume recruiters. Typically 8–12 candidates, one topic, 10–15 minutes, with two or three observers who say nothing.

**The critical insight:** a GD is not a debate and you do not win it. The evaluators are scoring how you behave in a group, not whether your side prevails.

---

## What they are actually scoring

| Criterion | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Content** | you say something substantive, not just agree loudly |
| **Clarity** | your point is understandable the first time |
| **Initiative** | you enter early rather than waiting to be invited |
| **Listening** | you reference and build on what others said |
| **Team behaviour** | you bring in a quiet person, defuse a clash |
| **Body language** | you look at the group, not at the evaluators |

> **The single most common failure is silence.** A candidate who says nothing cannot be
> scored and is eliminated by default. The second most common is dominating — talking over
> people scores worse than saying less.

---

## The three topic types

**1. Factual / current affairs.** "Should India move to a four-day work week?" Needs actual knowledge. Read a news summary daily for a month before placements — that is enough.

**2. Abstract.** "Black or white", "The wheel". No knowledge required; they are testing whether you can impose structure on something open-ended. Pick an interpretation in the first ten seconds and commit.

**3. Case-based.** A short scenario with a decision to make. Structure matters most: identify the problem, list options, weigh them, recommend.

---

## How to enter

**Starting the GD** is high risk and high reward. Do it only if you have something structured to say — a definition, a framing, or the two sides of the question. A weak opening is worse than a strong second contribution.

**A good opening:**
> "Before we take sides, it's worth separating two things: whether remote work helps
> productivity, and whether it helps employee wellbeing. They're often argued as one question
> but the evidence points different ways. I'll start with productivity."

**If you cannot start**, enter within the first two minutes. The longer you wait, the harder it gets and the less time you have to be scored.

**Getting in when everyone is talking:**
- Raise your hand slightly and lean forward — this is usually enough
- Use a bridge: *"Building on what Priya said…"*, *"Can I add a different angle to that…"*
- If two people clash, step in as the referee: *"I think you're both describing the same
  problem from different sides — can I try to put them together?"* This scores very well.

---

## What to say when you know nothing about the topic

You will not always know the subject. You can still score.

- **Structure the discussion.** "Let's define what we mean by X first." Framing is content.
- **Ask a sharpening question.** "Are we talking about India specifically or globally?"
- **Summarise.** "So far we've heard three arguments — cost, access, and quality. We haven't
  addressed access properly."
- **Build on someone.** Extend their point with a consequence or an example.

All four are scoreable contributions that require no prior knowledge.

---

## Concluding

If the group is asked for a conclusion, or you have a chance at the end, summarise **all** sides — not just yours.

> "We broadly agreed the technology has clear benefits in reach and cost. The main
> disagreement was on quality and equity of access, which nobody fully resolved. The
> practical position seems to be a hybrid model rather than either extreme."

Balanced summaries score highest. A conclusion that only restates your own view does not.

---

## Do and do not

**Do**
- Speak 3–5 times, in substantial contributions rather than one-liners
- Address the **group**, making eye contact around the circle
- Use names — it signals you were listening
- Bring in someone who has not spoken: *"Rahul, you've been quiet — what do you think?"*
  This is one of the highest-scoring single actions in a GD
- Keep each contribution to about 30–45 seconds
- Stay calm if contradicted

**Do not**
- Interrupt or talk over someone
- Get personal, aggressive or sarcastic
- Repeat a point already made
- Look at the evaluators while speaking
- Change your position just because you are outnumbered
- Fidget, tap the table, or slouch
- Use slang, or switch languages mid-sentence

## Common topics to prepare

**Current affairs / policy**
- AI and jobs in India · Work from home vs office · Four-day work week
- Should coding be compulsory in schools · Online vs classroom education
- Social media regulation · Data privacy · Electric vehicles in India
- Startup culture vs corporate jobs · Reservation in the private sector
- India's demographic dividend · Digital payments and financial inclusion

**Abstract**
- Black or white · The wheel · Water · Zero · Left or right · A blank page

**Case-based**
- A team member is underperforming near a deadline. What do you do?
- Your product has a defect found after launch. Recall or patch?
- Two projects, one team, both urgent. How do you allocate?

**Preparation method:** for each current-affairs topic, write **two points for, two against, and one balanced conclusion**. That is 30 minutes of work and covers most GDs you will face.

## Practice set

#### Q1. You have no knowledge of the topic. Name three things you can still do that score.

Define/frame the terms · ask a sharpening question · summarise the arguments so far. Building on someone's point is a fourth.

#### Q2. Two candidates are arguing loudly and going in circles. What is the highest-scoring move?

Step in as a synthesiser — restate both positions neutrally and merge them. Evaluators specifically look for this.

#### Q3. Is starting the GD always a good idea?

No. Start only with a structured opening — a definition or a framing. A weak start is worse than a strong contribution at minute two.

#### Q4. You have spoken once in ten minutes. What is your score likely to be?

Very low. Silence cannot be assessed; aim for 3–5 substantial contributions.

#### Q5. Everyone in the group disagrees with your position. Do you switch?

Not merely because you are outnumbered. Hold the position if you have reasons, acknowledge theirs, and refine rather than abandon. Consistency under pressure is scored.

#### Q6. Where should you look while speaking?

At the **group**, moving your gaze around it. Looking at the evaluators signals you are performing rather than discussing.

#### Q7. The topic is "Black or White" — pure abstract. How do you open?

Pick an interpretation immediately and declare it. For instance: treat it as a question about whether real decisions are ever binary, then argue that most are grey — and use examples.  
Committing to a frame *is* the skill being tested.

## Checkpoints

- [ ] I understand a GD is scored on group behaviour, not on winning
- [ ] I will speak within the first two minutes, every time
- [ ] I have practised entering a busy discussion with a bridge phrase
- [ ] I know the four things I can contribute even with zero topic knowledge
- [ ] I know that bringing in a quiet participant scores highly
- [ ] I can give a balanced closing summary covering all sides
- [ ] I have prepared two-for, two-against and a conclusion on ten current-affairs topics
- [ ] I have practised at least one GD out loud with friends
- [ ] I keep each contribution to 30–45 seconds and speak 3–5 times
