# HR interview

The last filter, and the one people prepare least. By this point the company has already decided you can do the work — the HR round is about **whether you will join, stay, and fit**.

Almost every question below is really asking one of three things:

1. **Will you accept the offer and actually turn up?**
2. **Will you leave within a year?**
3. **Are you someone the team can work with?**

Answer the real question, not the literal one.

---

## Tell me about yourself

The most-asked question in existence, and the one most often wasted. It is not an invitation to recite your biography.

**The structure — present, past, future, in 60–90 seconds:**

1. **Present** — who you are now, in one line
2. **Past** — two or three things that led here: a project, an internship, a skill
3. **Future** — why this role is the logical next step

> "I'm a final-year Computer Science student at DIT University, Dehradun. Over the last two
> years I've focused on backend development — I built a local judge system that compiles and
> runs code against hidden test cases, which taught me a lot about process handling and
> writing software people actually use daily. I also interned at [X], where I worked on
> [Y]. I'm looking for a role where I can keep building backend systems at a larger scale,
> which is why this position interested me."

**Rules:** keep it professional (no family details unless asked), end on why you are *here*, and stop talking. Do not fill silence.

## The core questions

### Why do you want to join our company?

They are testing whether you have done any homework. Generic praise is a fail.

**Prepare three specifics** about the company: something they build, a value or programme they run, and how it connects to what you want. One sentence each.

> "Three things. You do a lot of work in cloud migration, which is exactly the area I want to
> grow in. Your training programme has a reputation for taking freshers into real projects
> quickly rather than keeping them on the bench. And the people I've spoken to from my
> college who joined last year described mentorship that actually happens."

**Never say:** "It's a great company", "for a good salary", "everyone applies here".

### Why should we hire you?

Match yourself to the role, do not list adjectives.

> "You need someone who can pick up a codebase quickly and write reliable code. I've done
> that — [one concrete example]. I also learn fast; I taught myself [X] in a month for
> [project]. I'm not the most experienced person you'll interview, but I'll be one of the
> quickest to become useful."

### What are your strengths?

Pick **two**, each with evidence. Choose strengths relevant to the job.

> "Persistence and clarity. Persistence — when the judge I built kept failing on one compiler
> quirk, I spent three days isolating it rather than working around it. Clarity — I'm the
> person on my team who writes the documentation, because I find explaining something is how
> I check I actually understand it."

### What is your weakness?

**Do not** use a disguised strength ("I work too hard"). Interviewers have heard it and it reads as evasive. Give a real, non-fatal weakness **plus what you are doing about it**.

> "I used to over-engineer. On my first big project I built configuration options nobody ever
> used, and it made the code harder to change. I've started asking 'is this needed now?'
> before adding anything, and I've got much better at shipping the simple version first."

**Never name a weakness that is core to the job.** "I'm bad at logic" in a developer interview ends the conversation.

### Where do you see yourself in five years?

They are checking flight risk. Show ambition **within** their world.

> "Technically deep rather than moving away from engineering — I'd like to be the person the
> team asks about a particular system. Five years is realistically enough to become a senior
> engineer and start mentoring juniors, and that's the path I want."

**Never say:** "doing an MBA", "starting my own company", "in your chair" — all three say  
*I will leave*.

### Are you willing to relocate / work night shifts?

Say **yes** if it is true. If you have a genuine constraint, state it once, calmly, without apologising. Hedging is worse than a clear no — service companies place across many cities, and an evasive answer here often costs the offer outright.

### Are you comfortable with the service agreement / bond?

Read the terms beforehand and answer factually. "Yes, I've read that it's a two-year agreement and I'm comfortable with it" is the answer if it is true.

### Do you have any offers in hand?

Be honest but neutral. If yes: "I do, but I'm evaluating based on the work and the team, not just the offer." Never use another offer as leverage in a campus interview.

## Behavioural questions — use STAR

*Tell me about a time when you… failed / disagreed with someone / worked under pressure / led a team.*

> **S**ituation — the context, one sentence
> **T**ask — what you had to do
> **A**ction — what **you** specifically did (most of your answer)
> **R**esult — the outcome, and what you learned

**Prepare four stories in advance** and reuse them across questions:

1. A project you are proud of
2. A time you failed or made a mistake
3. A conflict or disagreement you resolved
4. A time you learned something hard, quickly

### Worked example — "Tell me about a time you failed"

> **S:** In my third semester I led a four-person team building a hostel management app.
> **T:** I was responsible for splitting the work and integrating everything.
> **A:** I split by feature but never agreed on a data format between us. Two days before
> submission, nothing integrated. I stayed up and wrote an adapter layer to translate between
> the two formats, and we submitted on time — but the code was ugly.
> **R:** We got the marks, but I learned that the interface between people's work matters more
> than the work itself. On every project since, the first thing I do is agree the data
> contract before anyone writes code.

**Note what makes it work:** a real failure, ownership without excuses, a concrete fix, and a lesson that changed later behaviour.

## The project question

*Explain your project.* Almost guaranteed if you have one on your resume.

**Structure:** the problem → what you built → your specific contribution → the hardest part → what you would change.

**Be ready for:** "Why did you choose that technology?", "What would you do differently?",  
"What was the hardest bug?", "How would you scale it?"

> **Never put anything on your resume you cannot defend.** If your resume says "React", expect
> React questions. Removing a line is safer than being caught out on it.

---

## Questions to ask them

When they say "Do you have any questions?", saying no reads as disinterest. Have **two** ready.

**Good:**
- "What does the first six months look like for a fresher here?"
- "How are freshers assigned to projects — is there a choice?"
- "What separates someone who does well here from someone who struggles?"
- "What is the team I'd be joining working on right now?"

**Avoid in the first round:** salary, appraisal cycles, leave policy, work-from-home. Those come after an offer.

## Practice set

#### Q1. "Tell me about yourself." What three parts should the answer have, in order?

**Present, past, future** — who you are now, what led here, why this role next. 60–90 seconds.

#### Q2. Why is "I work too hard" a bad answer to the weakness question?

It is a disguised strength. It reads as evasive and signals you are unwilling to be honest — which is itself the trait they are screening for.

#### Q3. What is wrong with answering "in five years I'd like to start my own company"?

The question tests flight risk. That answer says you intend to leave.

#### Q4. A company asks if you are willing to relocate. You would prefer not to, but you would. What do you say?

**Yes.** Hedging costs offers. If you would genuinely accept relocation, say so plainly;  
save preferences for after the offer.

#### Q5. Expand STAR and say which letter should take the most time.

Situation, Task, **Action**, Result. **Action** — they are assessing what *you* did.

#### Q6. What is the risk of listing a technology on your resume that you used once?

The interviewer will ask about it. An unsupported resume line does more damage than a shorter resume.

#### Q7. They ask "Do you have any questions for us?" Why is "no" the wrong answer?

It reads as disinterest in the role. Have two prepared questions about the work or the first six months.

#### Q8. You are asked about a conflict with a teammate. What must your answer avoid?

Blaming the other person. Describe the disagreement neutrally, then focus on what you did to resolve it.

## Traps

- Reciting your resume when asked "tell me about yourself".
- Generic answers to "why our company" — the fastest way to look uninterested.
- A fake weakness.
- Naming a weakness central to the job.
- Answers that imply you will leave: higher studies, own startup, "better opportunities".
- Talking over the interviewer, or filling silence after you have finished. Stop and let them
  ask.
- Badmouthing a college, professor or previous employer. It always reads badly.
- Asking about salary or leave in the first round.

## Checkpoints

- [ ] I have a 60–90 second "tell me about yourself" written and rehearsed aloud
- [ ] I have three company-specific reasons prepared for "why us", for each target company
- [ ] I have two strengths with evidence, and one honest weakness with a fix
- [ ] I have a five-year answer that keeps me inside the company
- [ ] I have decided my honest answer on relocation, shifts and the service agreement
- [ ] I have four STAR stories written out: proud project, failure, conflict, fast learning
- [ ] I can explain every project on my resume including its hardest bug
- [ ] I can defend every technology listed on my resume
- [ ] I have two questions ready to ask them
- [ ] I have said all of the above out loud at least once, not just read it
