# Assumptions, conclusions, arguments

Accenture asks 2–3 statement–conclusion and 2–3 argument/assumption questions every cycle;  
Infosys asks statement & conclusion. These feel subjective but are not — each has a mechanical test.

---

# Part 1 — Statement and assumption

An **assumption** is something the speaker must be **taking for granted** for the statement to make sense. It is unstated but necessary.

## The negation test — the only tool you need

> **Negate the assumption. If the statement collapses, the assumption is implicit. If the
> statement still stands, it is not.**

### Worked example

*Statement: "Use Brand X soap for soft, glowing skin." — an advertisement.*

*Assumption I: People want soft, glowing skin.*
- Negate it: "People do not want soft, glowing skin." The advertisement becomes pointless.
  → **implicit** ✓

*Assumption II: Brand X is the only soap that gives soft skin.*
- Negate it: "Other soaps also give soft skin." The ad still makes sense — it is still
  advertising a benefit. → **not implicit** ✗

## What is never an assumption

- Anything **already stated** in the statement (that is a fact, not an assumption)
- Anything **too strong**: words like *only*, *all*, *never*, *always*, *best*
- Anything about the **future** that the statement does not depend on
- Comparisons the statement does not make

> If an option contains "only", "all", "must always", or "the best", it is nearly always
> **not** an assumption. Advertisers and speakers rarely need such strong claims to hold.

# Part 2 — Statement and conclusion

A **conclusion** is what **must** follow from the statement — nothing more.

## The test

> **Could the statement be true while the conclusion is false?** If yes, it does not follow.

### Worked example

*Statement: "The company's profits rose 30% this year after it launched its online store."*

*Conclusion I: The online store caused the profit rise.*
- The statement says "after", not "because". Correlation is not causation. → **does not
  follow** ✗

*Conclusion II: The company made a profit this year.*
- Profits rose by 30%, so there were profits. → **follows** ✓

## The rules

- Stay **inside** the statement. No outside knowledge, no plausible extras.
- **"After" is not "because."** This single distinction decides many of these questions.
- A conclusion that is *probably* true still does not follow. It must be *necessarily* true.
- Beware conclusions that are **more extreme** than the statement.

# Part 3 — Course of action

*Statement describes a problem. Which suggested actions should follow?*

A course of action is appropriate if it is:

1. **Relevant** — it addresses the stated problem
2. **Practical** — it can actually be implemented
3. **Proportionate** — not absurdly drastic

### Worked example

*Statement: Road accidents have increased sharply on the highway near the city.*

*I. Speed limits should be enforced with cameras on that stretch.* → relevant, practical, proportionate → **follows** ✓

*II. The highway should be closed permanently.* → disproportionate; it destroys the road's purpose → **does not follow** ✗

> Reject any action that is extreme, that bans something outright, or that solves the
> problem by eliminating the thing itself.

# Part 4 — Strong and weak arguments

*Should India do X? Argue yes or no.*

A **strong** argument is:

- **Directly related** to the question asked
- Based on **facts or established reasoning**, not on personal opinion
- Addressing a **substantial** aspect, not a trivial one

A **weak** argument is:

- **Irrelevant** to the exact question
- Based on an assumption, an example, or an emotional appeal
- **Ambiguous** or circular ("Yes, because it should be done")
- Comparing to other countries without a reason why the comparison holds

### Worked example

*Question: Should coding be made compulsory in Indian schools?*

*Argument I: Yes, because computational thinking improves problem-solving across subjects and the economy increasingly requires digital skills.*  
→ relevant, substantial, reasoned → **strong** ✓

*Argument II: No, because children already have too many subjects.*  
→ raises a real constraint but does not engage with the value of the proposal; it is a generic objection that would apply to any new subject → **weak** ✗

*Argument III: Yes, because developed countries do it.*  
→ appeal to imitation with no reason given → **weak** ✗

# Part 5 — Inference

An **inference** sits between a fact and a conclusion: something you can reasonably deduce from the passage, though it is not stated.

Standard answer options:

| Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Definitely true** | it follows from the passage |
| **Probably true** | likely, but the passage does not guarantee it |
| **Data inadequate** | the passage says nothing either way |
| **Probably false** / **Definitely false** | the mirror cases |

**"Data inadequate" is a real answer and is under-selected.** If the passage genuinely does not address the point, choose it rather than reasoning from general knowledge.

## Practice set

### Assumptions

#### Q1. Statement: "Please switch off the lights when you leave the room." — a notice in an office.

I. Employees will read and follow the notice.  
II. Electricity is expensive.  
Negate I: employees will not read it → the notice is pointless → **implicit** ✓  
Negate II: electricity is cheap → the notice could still exist for environmental reasons →  
**not implicit** ✗  
**Only I is implicit.**

#### Q2. Statement: "Take this medicine twice daily to recover quickly." — a doctor to a patient.

I. The patient wants to recover quickly.  
II. This is the only medicine that works.  
Negating I destroys the advice → **implicit** ✓. II contains "only" → **not implicit** ✗  
**Only I.**

#### Q3. Statement: "Buy our insurance plan and secure your family's future."

I. People are concerned about their family's future.  
II. Insurance is the best way to secure a family's future.  
I is necessary → **implicit** ✓. II says "best" — too strong → ✗  
**Only I.**

#### Q4. Statement: "The government has decided to increase the tax on petrol."

I. The government needs additional revenue.  
II. People will stop using petrol entirely.  
I is a plausible necessary premise → **implicit** ✓  
II is extreme and not required → ✗  
**Only I.**

### Conclusions

#### Q5. Statement: "Sales of electric two-wheelers grew 60% last year, the fastest of any vehicle category."

I. Electric two-wheelers are now the most sold vehicle category.  
II. Electric two-wheeler sales increased last year.  
I confuses **growth rate** with **absolute volume** → ✗  
II is directly stated → ✓  
**Only II follows.**

#### Q6. Statement: "All employees who completed the training received a certificate. Ravi received a certificate."

I. Ravi completed the training.  
II. Some employees completed the training.  
I is the **converse error** — others might get certificates by another route → ✗  
II follows if any completed; the statement implies the training happened and certificates were issued for it → ✓  
**Only II follows.**

#### Q7. Statement: "The company's revenue doubled after it hired a new CEO."

I. The new CEO caused the revenue growth.  
II. The company's revenue increased.  
"After" is not "because" → I ✗. II is stated → ✓  
**Only II follows.**

### Course of action

#### Q8. Statement: Many students in the district fail mathematics every year.

I. Remedial mathematics classes should be arranged.  
II. Mathematics should be removed from the syllabus.  
I is relevant, practical and proportionate → ✓  
II removes the subject rather than solving the problem → ✗  
**Only I follows.**

#### Q9. Statement: A city's air quality has deteriorated sharply in winter.

I. Construction dust and stubble burning should be regulated and monitored.  
II. All vehicles should be permanently banned from the city.  
I addresses the causes proportionately ✓. II is disproportionate ✗  
**Only I follows.**

### Strong / weak arguments

#### Q10. Should India invest heavily in solar power?

I. Yes — India receives high solar irradiance year-round and the cost per unit has fallen below coal in many states.  
II. No — because solar panels are manufactured abroad.  
I is relevant, factual and substantial → **strong**  
II raises a point but does not engage with whether the investment is worthwhile; it also ignores that manufacturing can be localised → **weak**

#### Q11. Should aptitude tests be used in campus hiring?

I. Yes — they provide a uniform standard when firms cannot interview thousands of applicants.  
II. Yes — because every big company already does it.  
I gives a genuine reason ✓ **strong**  
II is an appeal to popularity ✗ **weak**

### Inference

#### Q12. Passage: "The state's literacy rate rose from 68% to 79% over the decade, though rural female literacy still trails the state average by 12 points."

I. Literacy improved in the state. → **definitely true**  
II. Rural female literacy also improved. → **data inadequate** (the passage gives only the current gap, not its trend)  
III. The state has achieved full literacy. → **definitely false**

---

## Traps

- Bringing in real-world knowledge. Everything must come from the given text.
- Confusing an assumption (unstated, necessary, *before* the statement) with a conclusion
  (follows *after* the statement).
- Options with absolute words — *only, all, never, best* — are usually wrong for assumptions
  and conclusions alike.
- Treating "probably true" as "definitely true".
- In arguments, a *true* statement can still be a *weak* argument if it does not bear on the
  question asked. Relevance beats truth here.

---

## Checkpoints

- [ ] I use the negation test for every assumption question
- [ ] I reject options containing "only", "all", "never" or "best" as assumptions
- [ ] I know an assumption is unstated but necessary, never something already stated
- [ ] I test conclusions by asking whether the statement could be true while the conclusion is false
- [ ] I never treat "after" as "because"
- [ ] I judge a course of action on relevance, practicality and proportionality
- [ ] I judge an argument on relevance and substance, not on whether I agree with it
- [ ] I am willing to select "data inadequate" when the passage genuinely does not say
